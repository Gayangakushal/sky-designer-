# How to Choose a Social Media Management Agency in Sri Lanka

Choose a social media management agency by comparing the complete operating system behind the posts: strategy, content planning, creative production, publishing, community responsibilities, approvals and reporting. The right provider should understand the business, protect brand consistency and make responsibilities clear. A portfolio alone is not enough, and a high posting volume does not guarantee useful business outcomes.

This guide helps Sri Lankan businesses compare providers on scope, process and fit without assuming that every company needs the same channels or number of posts.

## Define what “social media management” means for your business

Social media management can describe very different scopes. One provider may create and publish content. Another may also develop strategy, respond to messages, produce video, coordinate offers and report on business actions.

Before requesting proposals, decide which problems need to be solved.

For example:

- The business posts inconsistently.
- Visuals and tone change from week to week.
- Content is produced without a clear audience or purpose.
- Approvals delay every post.
- Comments and messages have no owner.
- Reports show activity but not useful learning.
- Organic content and paid advertising responsibilities are confused.
- The internal team lacks time, specialist skills or a repeatable workflow.

A clear problem statement helps agencies propose an appropriate service rather than a generic monthly quantity.

## What does social media management normally include?

There is no universal package. A provider should state which of the following responsibilities are included and which remain with the client.

### Strategy and content planning

Content planning connects the business objective, audience and offer with repeatable themes. It should explain why the brand is publishing—not only when.

A useful planning process may define:

- the role of each selected channel;
- priority audiences and customer questions;
- content themes or pillars;
- campaign, promotional and evergreen needs;
- the balance of formats;
- publishing and approval timing;
- the actions or responses the team will review.

Ask the agency how it turns business information into a monthly plan and how that plan changes when evidence or priorities change.

### Copy and creative design

The proposal should explain who develops ideas, writes copy, designs visual assets and prepares format or language variations. If photography, filming, animation or advanced production is needed, confirm whether it is included or separately scoped.

Compare creative support based on:

- understanding of the brand and audience;
- clarity of the message;
- consistency across posts;
- suitability for the intended format;
- the purpose of each variation;
- the revision and approval process;
- ownership and delivery of completed files.

They should be assessed as portfolio examples, not as proof of reach, engagement or sales.

### Publishing and page management

Clarify whether the agency schedules or publishes approved content, which channels are included and who supplies access. Also confirm what happens when a post must change because of stock, pricing, an event, an operational issue or late approval.

Useful questions include:

- Who gives final approval?
- How far ahead is the calendar prepared?
- What is the cut-off for changes?
- Who checks links, dates, contact details and offer information?
- How are urgent corrections handled?
- What access will the agency receive, and who retains ownership?

A stable publishing process reduces errors and last-minute work, but it still needs a clear route for genuine exceptions.

### Community management

Community management can include monitoring and responding to comments or messages, hiding or escalating harmful content, and directing enquiries to the correct business team. It does not mean the agency can answer every product, service, complaint or sales question without client input.

Before hiring a provider, define:

- which interactions the agency may answer;
- the approved tone and response guidance;
- which questions require escalation;
- who handles sales, complaints or sensitive issues;
- expected service hours and response responsibilities;
- how enquiries are recorded and handed over;
- which languages can be supported accurately.

Do not assume that “page management” includes full customer service. Put the boundary in writing.

### Reporting and review

A report should help the business decide what to continue, change or investigate. It should not be a collection of screenshots without interpretation.

Ask how the agency will report:

- content published during the period;
- performance by useful content theme or format;
- audience responses and recurring questions;
- enquiries or website actions where they can be recorded;
- community-management issues;
- tests, limitations and next recommendations;
- activities that cannot be connected confidently to a business outcome.

Reporting should match the role social media plays for the business. A page used for customer questions needs different evidence from one focused on awareness or product education.

## Separate organic social management from paid media

Organic social media management and paid advertising can support each other, but they are not the same service.

Organic management usually focuses on the brand's ongoing presence: planning, content, publishing, community activity and learning from audience response. Paid media involves budgeted advertising, campaign decisions, creative testing and performance measurement against a defined objective.

Before comparing proposals, ask:

- Is paid campaign management included or separate?
- Is media spend included in the quoted total?
- Who creates advertising-specific variations?
- Are organic and paid results reported separately?
- Which team decides how content may be reused in advertising?
- Who owns the relevant accounts and data?

Keeping the scopes clear prevents a monthly content package from being mistaken for a complete advertising service.

## Look for brand consistency, not identical posts

Brand consistency means the audience can recognise the business and understand its message across different content types. It does not require every post to use the same layout.

An agency should be able to work with:

- the existing identity and brand guidelines;
- approved logos, colours, typography and imagery;
- the business's tone and terminology;
- different campaign messages within one system;
- language versions where genuinely required;
- practical templates for recurring content;
- rules for offers, claims and contact information.

Ask how the team documents creative decisions so consistency does not depend on one designer remembering previous posts.

If the underlying identity is unclear or inconsistent, the business may need a separate [Branding and Creative Design service](/services/branding-creative-design) rather than expecting social media templates to solve the entire brand problem.

## Examine the approval workflow before signing

Even strong creative work can fail operationally if nobody knows who supplies information, reviews copy or approves final files.

A practical workflow should identify:

1. when the client supplies offers, dates, product details and priorities;
2. when the agency presents the calendar or concepts;
3. who checks facts, prices and operational details;
4. who gives final approval;
5. how revisions are requested and recorded;
6. when approved content is scheduled or published;
7. what happens when approval is late;
8. how urgent changes are authorised.

Ask to see the proposed workflow and tools, but focus on responsibilities rather than the name of the software. The best tool cannot compensate for unclear ownership.

## Compare business understanding and strategic fit

An agency should learn enough about the business to make useful content decisions. Before proposing a calendar, it should ask about the offer, customers, seasonal priorities, sales path, brand restrictions and internal capacity.

Look for questions such as:

- What does the business need customers to understand?
- Which offers or services matter most?
- What questions do customers ask before buying?
- Which content or channels have already been useful?
- What must never be claimed or communicated?
- Who will supply product, operational and sales information?
- What happens after a social media enquiry arrives?

If a proposal is built entirely around post quantity before these questions are discussed, the content may be difficult to connect to business priorities.

## How do you choose a social media management agency?

Choose a social media management agency by comparing its strategy, content-planning process, creative scope, publishing and community responsibilities, approval workflow, reporting, ownership and paid-media boundaries. A useful proposal should explain both what the agency will manage and what information, approvals or customer follow-up the business must provide.

Use a consistent list when comparing providers.

### Strategy and scope

1. Which business objective will guide the content plan?
2. Which channels do you recommend, and what role will each play?
3. What is included in planning, copy, design, publishing and reporting?
4. Which production, tools or services are additional?

### Creative and approvals

5. Who develops ideas, copy and visual assets?
6. How many review stages or revisions are included?
7. Who checks factual information before publication?
8. How are urgent or late changes handled?

### Community and enquiries

9. Are comments or messages included in the service?
10. Which interactions will the agency answer or escalate?
11. How are enquiries handed to the business and recorded?
12. Which languages can the assigned team support accurately?

### Reporting and ownership

13. Which outcomes will appear in the report?
14. How will the agency explain what was learned?
15. Who owns the accounts, data and completed creative files?
16. What access and materials are returned when the engagement ends?

### Paid media boundary

17. Is advertising management included or separately scoped?
18. Is media spend separate from the service fee?
19. Who creates and approves advertising-specific content?

The quality of the answers matters more than the number of services promised.

## Common red flags

Ask for more clarity when a provider:

- promises guaranteed growth, engagement, leads or sales without a verifiable basis;
- recommends every available channel without explaining its role;
- sells only a post quantity with no audience, theme or business objective;
- cannot define publishing, approval or community responsibilities;
- presents portfolio visuals as proof of commercial performance;
- reports only followers, reach or likes without connecting them to the channel's role;
- treats paid advertising as included but does not separate media spend or campaign scope;
- is unclear about account access, data or creative ownership;
- relies heavily on copied trends without showing how they fit the brand;
- cannot explain what information the client must provide.

Not every missing detail is a reason to reject an agency. It is a reason to make the scope explicit before work begins.

## Prepare a better brief before requesting proposals

Agencies can respond more accurately when the business supplies the same core information to each provider.

Include:

- a short description of the business and priority offers;
- the main audience and locations served;
- the role social media should play;
- current channel links and available performance information;
- existing brand guidelines and assets;
- required languages and approval reviewers;
- available photography, video or product material;
- community and enquiry-handling expectations;
- important dates, seasonal needs and operational limits;
- whether paid advertising is part of the brief;
- the intended engagement period and decision timeline.

The brief does not need to prescribe the solution. It should give each agency enough context to propose one.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is normally included in social media management?

Depending on the provider, it may include strategy, content calendars, copy, creative design, publishing, page activity, community support and reporting. Production, advertising and customer-service responsibilities may be separate. Always compare the written scope.

### How many posts should a business publish each month?

There is no universal number. A sustainable plan depends on the audience, channel role, available ideas, production capacity, campaign priorities and ability to review results. Post quantity should follow the strategy rather than replace it.

### Is paid advertising part of social media management?

Sometimes, but not automatically. Confirm whether campaign management is included and keep media spend separate from the organic-management or agency fee. Advertising-specific creative and reporting may also require a separate scope.

### Who should approve social media content?

The business should name a responsible approver who can verify offers, dates, prices, claims and operational details. The agency can manage the workflow, but final factual authority should be clear.

### Should an agency answer every comment and message?

Only if the scope, response guidance, service hours, languages and escalation rules support it. Sales, complaints, sensitive matters and specialist questions often need a client-side owner.

### How can a business judge social media results?

Start with the role of the channel. Review content response, recurring audience questions, useful enquiries, website actions or other business evidence where available. Avoid treating follower or reach growth as the only definition of success.

## Choose the provider whose process the business can support

A social media agency needs more than design skill. It needs a clear content strategy, reliable production and approval workflow, defined community responsibilities, useful reporting and enough business understanding to make consistent decisions.

Compare providers on the complete operating model, not only the number of posts or the appearance of a portfolio. Make paid-media boundaries and account ownership explicit, and choose a workflow that the internal team can sustain.
