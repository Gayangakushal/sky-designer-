# How to Choose a Meta Ads Agency in Sri Lanka

The right Meta Ads agency should understand your business before it recommends a campaign. It should be able to explain the strategy, campaign structure, creative-testing plan, measurement approach and reporting process in language you can evaluate. A good comparison therefore goes beyond fees, promises or screenshots: it asks how each agency will connect advertising activity to lead or sales quality.

This guide provides a practical way to compare agencies without assuming that one provider, campaign structure or budget is right for every Sri Lankan business.

## Start with the business problem, not the advertising platform

Before comparing agencies, define what the advertising is expected to help the business achieve. “We need Facebook Ads” is a channel request, not yet a useful business objective.

A clearer brief might identify:

- the product, service or offer being promoted;
- the audience the business wants to reach;
- the action that matters, such as an enquiry, qualified conversation, booking or purchase;
- the sales process that follows that action;
- the campaign period or commercial deadline;
- the evidence currently available from the website, sales team or previous campaigns.

An agency should ask about these points before offering a confident solution. If the conversation begins and ends with reach, clicks or a proposed media budget, it may not yet address the full business problem.

## What should a Meta Ads agency actually help manage?

Scope varies between providers, so do not assume that “Meta Ads management” means the same thing in every proposal. Ask each agency to state what is included, excluded and dependent on the client.

A complete engagement may need to address several connected areas.

### Strategy and offer clarity

The agency should understand what is being sold, why a customer should care, and what happens after someone responds. Advertising cannot repair an unclear offer or an unavailable sales process on its own.

Look for an agency that can explain:

- the campaign's role in the wider customer journey;
- the audience problem or motivation being addressed;
- the offer and message being tested;
- the primary business outcome;
- the assumptions that still need evidence.

The explanation should be specific enough to evaluate but flexible enough to change when real results contradict the original assumption.

### Campaign structure

A campaign plan should show how budget, audience, creative and conversion paths relate to the stated objective. You do not need to accept a complicated structure simply because it sounds technical.

Ask the agency to explain:

1. what each part of the campaign is intended to learn or achieve;
2. how decisions will be made when performance differs;
3. which elements will remain stable during a test;
4. when the team will review or change the setup;
5. how the structure supports reporting that the business can understand.

Current platform options can change. The durable test is whether the agency can explain the reasoning behind the structure rather than treating one setup as a permanent rule.

### Creative testing

Creative testing should be a planned learning process, not an endless supply of unrelated artwork. A useful test compares a meaningful variable—such as the message, hook, offer presentation or format—while keeping enough of the surrounding conditions stable to interpret the result.

Questions to ask include:

- What question is this creative test meant to answer?
- Which variable is changing?
- What result will be used to compare versions?
- How will lead or sales quality be considered alongside attention metrics?
- What will happen after a useful result is found?

The agency should also explain who produces the creative, how many deliverables are included, who approves them, and whether production costs are separate from campaign management.

For a deeper explanation of the testing mindset, review Sky Designers' article on [using creative testing as a campaign learning tool](/blog/meta-creative-test-setup-real-scale-tool). Any feature-specific instructions in that article should be checked against current Meta documentation before use.

### Tracking and measurement

Tracking is not only a technical installation task. The business and agency must agree on what a meaningful outcome is and how it will be recorded.

Before appointing an agency, ask:

- Which actions will be measured?
- Who owns and controls the relevant accounts and data?
- How will website, form, call, message or sales information be connected where possible?
- What attribution limits should be understood?
- How will missing or incomplete data be reported?

An agency should not imply that every sale can always be attributed perfectly. It should state what the available evidence can show, what it cannot show, and which measurement gaps need improvement.

### Reporting and interpretation

A useful report does more than reproduce a platform dashboard. It connects activity to the campaign objective and explains what the team learned.

Ask to see the structure of a representative report with confidential information removed. It should make clear:

- the reporting period and objective;
- media spend and the relevant outcomes;
- the definitions behind reported leads, bookings or sales;
- creative- or audience-level findings where reliable;
- changes made during the period;
- measurement limitations;
- recommended next actions and the reason for them.

The agency should be willing to discuss weak results as clearly as strong ones. Transparency matters most when a campaign is not behaving as expected.

## Evaluate lead and sales quality, not only lead volume

A low cost per lead can look attractive while producing few suitable customers. The agency should therefore be interested in what happens after a person submits a form, starts a conversation or calls the business.

This does not mean the agency controls the entire sales process. It means the campaign should be evaluated with enough downstream information to distinguish attention from commercial value.

Agree on practical quality signals such as:

- whether the enquiry matches the intended customer profile;
- whether contact details are usable;
- whether the person understands the offer;
- whether the enquiry reaches a meaningful sales stage;
- whether a booking, registration or sale can be verified;
- why apparently interested prospects do not continue.

## Compare communication and decision-making

Campaign management involves repeated decisions. Before signing an agreement, understand how those decisions will be communicated.

Ask each provider:

- Who will be the day-to-day contact?
- Who makes strategic, creative and campaign decisions?
- How often are reviews held?
- What information must the client supply?
- How quickly are approvals expected?
- What happens when urgent creative, offer or sales issues appear?
- How are disagreements about performance handled?

Fast replies are useful, but communication quality is more important than message volume. Look for a team that can explain trade-offs clearly and record important decisions.

## Confirm transparency and account ownership

The proposal should identify who owns or controls the advertising account, business assets, creative files, tracking access and historical data. It should also explain what access remains available if the relationship ends.

Confirm in writing:

- which accounts will be used;
- what level of access each party receives;
- how media spend is separated from agency or service fees;
- who owns completed creative deliverables;
- whether any tools or production costs are additional;
- how data and reports will be handed over;
- the notice, renewal and termination terms.

These are commercial and operational questions, not merely technical ones. Clear ownership protects both the client and the agency.

## Ask for evidence you can evaluate

Evidence should be relevant to the service being considered. A polished visual portfolio can demonstrate creative capability, but it does not by itself prove campaign performance. A campaign screenshot can show a result, but it may not explain attribution, lead quality or the time period.

Useful evidence can include:

- an anonymised report showing how findings are explained;
- a case study with dates, spend, outcome definitions and limitations;
- examples of creative variations linked to a stated test;
- a description of how the team responded to weak performance;
- a real project relevant to the proposed deliverables;
- references that the agency has permission to share.

Do not ask an agency to expose another client's confidential data. Instead, look for enough context to understand how the team thinks and measures.

## Questions to ask before choosing an agency

Use the same core questions with every shortlisted provider so proposals are easier to compare.

1. What business outcome would you prioritise, and why?
2. What assumptions need to be tested first?
3. What is included in strategy, management, creative and reporting?
4. How will campaign and sales-quality information be connected?
5. What access and data ownership will our business retain?
6. How do you decide when to change a campaign?
7. How will weak or inconclusive results be communicated?
8. What information and response time do you need from our team?
9. Which costs are media spend, and which are service or production fees?
10. Which examples are genuinely comparable to our brief?

## Common red flags

Be cautious when a proposal or sales conversation relies on:

- guaranteed sales, leads or returns without a verified basis;
- results presented without dates, spend, definitions or attribution context;
- an unwillingness to explain account and data ownership;
- a campaign recommendation made before understanding the offer and sales journey;
- reporting focused only on reach, clicks or lead quantity;
- unclear boundaries between media spend, management fees and creative costs;
- a fixed “best” campaign structure presented as suitable for every business;
- no process for approvals, communication or learning from poor results.

A red flag is a reason to ask for clarity, not always proof that a provider is unsuitable. The quality of the response can be revealing.

## A simple agency comparison scorecard

| Area | What to compare |
| --- | --- |
| Business understanding | Quality of questions about the offer, audience, customer journey and sales process |
| Strategy | Clear objective, assumptions and priorities rather than a list of platform tasks |
| Campaign structure | Reasoned setup, decision rules and understandable responsibilities |
| Creative testing | Test questions, production scope, approval process and quality measurement |
| Tracking | Outcome definitions, access, attribution limits and data gaps |
| Reporting | Business interpretation, transparency and next actions |
| Lead/sales quality | Process for connecting campaign responses with downstream evidence |
| Communication | Named contacts, review rhythm, escalation and decision ownership |
| Commercial clarity | Separation of media, service, creative and additional costs |
| Evidence | Relevant, contextual and permission-safe examples |

Score each area against the needs of your business rather than adding every row with equal weight. A business with a complex sales handoff may prioritise tracking and lead quality; a brand launching a new offer may need stronger strategic and creative support.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a Meta Ads agency provide?

The scope may include strategy, campaign setup and management, creative planning or production, testing, tracking support, reporting and review. Providers package these responsibilities differently, so the proposal should state exactly what is included and what the client must supply.

### Should an agency guarantee a specific result?

Be cautious with guarantees that do not have a clear, verifiable basis. Advertising results depend on the offer, audience, creative, competition, budget, customer journey, sales process and measurement quality. An agency can commit to a process and deliverables more reliably than it can guarantee a commercial outcome.

### Who should own the advertising account and data?

Account, access and data ownership should be agreed before work begins. The business should understand which assets it controls, what the agency can access, and what happens to historical information and creative files when the engagement ends.

### How long should a business wait before evaluating an agency?

There is no universal period. The campaign objective, budget, sales cycle, available data and testing plan all affect when a conclusion is reasonable. Ask the agency to define the review points and decision criteria before launch.

### Is the lowest management fee the best choice?

Not necessarily. Compare scope, creative responsibilities, measurement, communication, evidence and commercial clarity. A lower fee may suit a business with strong internal capabilities, while another business may need broader support.

## Choose the agency whose reasoning you can trust

Choosing a Meta Ads agency is ultimately a decision about strategy, evidence and working relationships. The strongest proposal should show that the team understands the business problem, can explain its decisions, respects account ownership and will evaluate lead or sales quality rather than hide behind surface-level metrics.
