# How to Choose a Web Design Company in Sri Lanka

Choose a web design company by evaluating how well it understands the business objective, plans the user journey, handles mobile experience and SEO foundations, and defines ownership and support. A strong visual portfolio matters, but it is not enough. The proposal should also explain content, CMS access, hosting, security, performance, analytics, maintenance and what happens after launch.

The right provider is not necessarily the company with the longest feature list. It is the one whose process, evidence and responsibilities fit the website your business actually needs.

## Define what the website must achieve

Before comparing providers, write a short business brief:

- Who are the priority users?
- What must they understand?
- What action should they take?
- Which products, services or locations need pages?
- What evidence helps users trust the business?
- Who will provide and approve content?
- Which systems must connect to the site?
- Who will update it after launch?
- How will useful actions be measured?

This prevents a supplier comparison from becoming a contest over design trends or page quantities.

## Review the portfolio as evidence, not decoration

Do not judge a portfolio only by whether you like its colours. Open relevant websites and examine the problem each project appears to solve.

Review:

- clarity of the offer;
- navigation and page hierarchy;
- mobile presentation;
- readability and accessibility;
- forms, calls to action and error states;
- content depth;
- consistency across pages;
- signs of custom thinking versus repeated layouts;
- whether the live experience still works as presented.

Ask the provider to explain its actual responsibility: strategy, copy, design, development, content entry, integrations, hosting or maintenance. A screenshot cannot reveal who completed each part.

## Evaluate mobile experience directly

“Responsive” should mean more than shrinking a desktop page. Test portfolio sites on a phone and inspect:

- navigation and menu access;
- text size and line length;
- tap targets;
- form usability;
- image and video behaviour;
- tables or comparison content;
- sticky elements and pop-ups;
- page speed under ordinary conditions;
- whether the most important action remains clear.

Ask which devices and browsers are included in testing and how mobile decisions are reviewed before launch.

## Ask how SEO fundamentals are handled

A website provider should distinguish technical and content foundations from ongoing SEO work.

Relevant foundations may include:

- crawlable navigation and pages;
- logical headings;
- editable SEO titles and descriptions;
- canonical URLs;
- sitemap and robots handling;
- redirects during a redesign;
- internal links;
- structured data where appropriate;
- useful image alt text;
- performance and accessibility considerations;
- analytics and search-verification access where included.

These elements do not guarantee rankings. Ask what is included, what remains the client's responsibility and what ongoing content or SEO support is separate.

## Confirm CMS and editing responsibilities

If staff need to update content, ask for a demonstration of the proposed editing experience.

Confirm:

- which content can be edited;
- which changes require development support;
- user roles and permissions;
- training and documentation;
- content backup or export;
- software/dependency maintenance;
- how mistakes can be corrected;
- access after the provider relationship ends.

The most familiar CMS is not automatically the best option. The choice should reflect editing needs, maintenance, security and ownership.

## Put ownership and access in writing

Before appointing a provider, identify who owns or controls:

- the domain registration;
- hosting account;
- website code or platform account;
- design and content files;
- CMS administrator access;
- analytics and search tools;
- customer or form data;
- licensed fonts, images, software or plugins;
- backups;
- third-party integrations.

Ask what is handed over at launch and termination. A business should not discover later that essential access depends on an unavailable supplier.

## Separate hosting from website development

Hosting is an ongoing operational service. Website development is the project that creates or changes the site. They may be supplied together, but the proposal should separate their responsibilities.

Ask:

- Where will the site be hosted?
- Who owns and pays for the account?
- Who manages renewals?
- What backup and restore process applies?
- How are certificates and environment settings managed?
- Who responds to hosting incidents?
- Can the site be moved, and what is required?
- Is email hosting separate?

Do not accept “hosting included” without understanding duration, ownership and renewal.

## Understand maintenance and support

Maintenance may include updates, backups, security review, bug fixes, content support, monitoring or integration work. “Support” can mean anything from answering questions to resolving defined incidents.

Confirm:

- what maintenance is included;
- what begins after launch;
- how issues are reported and prioritised;
- whether content changes are covered;
- which third-party failures are outside the provider's control;
- how additional work is approved;
- what records or reports the business receives;
- how handover works if support ends.

Avoid assuming a standard response time or warranty. Use the provider's written terms.

## Ask practical security questions

No provider can promise that a website will never be attacked or fail. A credible provider should still explain how risk is handled.

Questions include:

- How are administrator accounts protected?
- Who receives the highest level of access?
- How are software and dependencies maintained?
- How are backups created and restore processes checked?
- How are forms and stored customer data handled?
- What happens when a security issue is reported?
- Which security responsibilities belong to hosting or external services?
- What must the client's staff do?

Security claims should be specific to the proposed technology and operating arrangement.

## Examine performance as a user experience issue

Performance affects usability, particularly when pages contain large images, video, scripts or complex interfaces.

Ask how the provider approaches:

- image and media optimization;
- unnecessary scripts and dependencies;
- loading behaviour;
- fonts and third-party resources;
- mobile testing;
- caching and hosting configuration;
- performance checks before launch;
- monitoring after launch, if included.

A laboratory score is useful evidence but not the only user-experience measure. Ask how issues will be prioritised and which factors are outside the project scope.

## Define analytics before development ends

Analytics should reflect the site's purpose. Decide which actions matter, such as enquiries, bookings, calls, downloads or purchases where applicable.

Confirm:

- which analytics setup is included;
- event definitions;
- consent and privacy responsibilities;
- who owns and accesses the account;
- how implementation is tested;
- who monitors whether tracking continues working;
- how website actions connect to offline outcomes;
- attribution limitations.

Installing a tool without a measurement plan does not create useful reporting.

## Compare the complete project process

A reliable proposal should explain:

1. discovery and requirements;
2. sitemap and content responsibilities;
3. design decisions and approvals;
4. development and integrations;
5. content entry or migration;
6. responsive, functional and browser testing;
7. SEO and analytics foundations;
8. launch responsibilities;
9. training and handover;
10. maintenance and support boundaries.

Ask who owns each stage and what the client must provide. Avoid assuming a standard timeline; the schedule must follow the approved scope and dependencies.

## Questions to ask before hiring a web design company

### Strategy and scope

1. What business and user problem will the site solve?
2. What sitemap and page types are included?
3. What is custom, and what uses an existing system or template?
4. Who supplies, writes and approves content?

### Technology and ownership

5. Which CMS or platform is proposed, and why?
6. Who owns the domain, hosting, code, accounts and data?
7. Which licences or third-party services are required?
8. What access and files are handed over?

### Quality and launch

9. How are mobile, accessibility, security and performance reviewed?
10. Which SEO and analytics foundations are included?
11. How are redirects and existing URLs protected during a redesign?
12. Who checks forms, integrations and error states?

### Ongoing operation

13. What maintenance is required?
14. What support is included after launch?
15. How are backups and restores handled?
16. What happens if the relationship ends?

## Common red flags

Ask for clarification when a provider:

- recommends a solution before understanding users and business actions;
- cannot explain ownership or administrator access;
- presents SEO as a guaranteed ranking;
- treats mobile review as an afterthought;
- cannot separate hosting, maintenance and development responsibilities;
- relies only on screenshots instead of accessible live work or project context;
- does not define content and migration responsibilities;
- is vague about licences, recurring services or third-party dependencies;
- offers security guarantees without an operating process;
- has no testing, launch or handover checklist.

## A provider comparison scorecard

| Area | Evidence to compare |
| --- | --- |
| Business understanding | Questions, requirements and user-journey reasoning |
| Portfolio relevance | Live, comparable work and clear provider responsibility |
| Mobile UX | Navigation, forms, content hierarchy and real-device review |
| SEO foundations | Crawlability, metadata, redirects, structure and ownership |
| CMS | Editing fit, roles, training and maintenance |
| Ownership | Domain, hosting, accounts, data, code and licences |
| Quality | Accessibility, security, performance and testing process |
| Measurement | Relevant events, analytics access and limitations |
| Support | Maintenance scope, issue process and handover |
| Commercial clarity | Deliverables, exclusions, recurring costs and change control |

Weight the criteria according to the project. Ecommerce operations may make integrations and security especially important; a service site may depend more on content and enquiry journeys.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I choose the cheapest website quotation?

Not without comparing scope. Content, custom design, integrations, SEO foundations, ownership, hosting, maintenance and support may differ substantially between proposals.

### Should the business own the domain and hosting?

The business should understand and agree on ownership and access. Confirm who controls renewals, administrator accounts, billing, backups and handover.

### Does every website need a CMS?

No. A CMS is useful when approved users need to update content. It also creates training, permission, maintenance and security responsibilities that should match the business's needs.

### Can a web design company guarantee SEO rankings?

No responsible provider can guarantee rankings. It can implement agreed technical and content foundations, but search performance also depends on relevance, competition, authority, ongoing work and other factors.

### What support is needed after launch?

That depends on the technology and business. Define updates, backups, security, bugs, content, integrations, analytics and incident responsibilities in the agreement.

### How can I verify website performance claims?

Review relevant live work, ask what the provider controlled and request evidence with scope and context. Do not treat one score or screenshot as proof of every future result.

A successful website project needs more than an attractive design. It needs clear content, responsive user journeys, appropriate technology, ownership, testing, measurement and an operating plan after launch.
