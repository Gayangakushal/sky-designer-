# How Much Does a Business Website Cost in Sri Lanka?

There is no single business website price in Sri Lanka. Cost changes according to the type of site, number and complexity of pages, custom design, content, content-management needs, ecommerce, integrations, SEO requirements and ongoing ownership. A small information site and a custom ecommerce platform should not be compared by page count alone.

To budget responsibly, define what the website must help users do, what the business must manage after launch and which costs are initial versus ongoing.

## What affects website development cost in Sri Lanka?

Website development cost depends on the site type, page and content structure, custom design, CMS, ecommerce features, integrations, responsive behaviour, testing, SEO requirements and support. Separate the initial design and development scope from content work, domain and hosting, third-party services, maintenance and other recurring costs before comparing quotations or deciding which proposal offers better value.

## Start with the business job of the website

A website is not only a collection of pages. It should help a defined audience understand the offer, trust the business and complete a useful next action.

Before requesting quotations, clarify:

- who the priority users are;
- what they need to understand;
- what action they should take;
- which content or evidence supports that decision;
- which internal process follows an enquiry, booking or order;
- who will update the site after launch;
- which systems must connect to it;
- which languages or accessibility requirements apply;
- how performance and outcomes will be reviewed.

The clearer the job, the easier it is to avoid paying for unnecessary features or discovering missing requirements after development begins.

## Common website types and how their scope differs

These categories overlap; they are not fixed packages.

### Brochure or business information website

This type of site usually explains the business, services, evidence and contact path. Cost depends on content depth, custom design, forms, languages, editing needs and the number of distinct page templates—not merely the number of navigation links.

The published [Lulu Bridal Studio & Academy website project](/work/lulu-bridal-studio-academy-website) is an example of a service-led site presenting bridal services, academy programmes, collections and appointment information. Its scope should not be assumed for another business.

### Custom website

A custom site may require a distinctive interface, tailored content structures, specialised interactions, account features or business-specific workflows. Custom does not mean adding visual effects without purpose. It means the design or development responds to requirements that a simpler structure cannot meet well.

### Ecommerce website

Ecommerce includes more than displaying products. Requirements can involve catalogues, variants, stock, checkout, payments, delivery, customer communication, order management, policies and ongoing product administration.

The [Lustre & Legacy ecommerce website project](/work/lustre-legacy-luxury-gemstone-e-commerce-website) is a verified published project example. It does not establish a universal ecommerce feature list, cost or result.

### Landing page or campaign experience

A focused landing page may support one offer or campaign. Its scope can still include strategy, copy, design, forms, tracking, integration and testing. A single page is not automatically simple.

## Cost factor 1: information architecture and page scope

Page count affects effort, but page type and content complexity matter more.

Consider:

- unique page templates versus repeated content entries;
- service, product, location or category structures;
- navigation and user journeys;
- filters, search or comparison needs;
- forms and conditional fields;
- legal and policy content;
- multilingual versions;
- migration of existing pages and URLs.

Ask the provider to show a sitemap and identify the distinct templates or content types included.

## Cost factor 2: custom design and user experience

Design work can include:

- discovery and user-journey planning;
- wireframes or content hierarchy;
- visual direction;
- responsive layouts;
- interaction states;
- forms, errors and confirmations;
- accessibility considerations;
- review and revision stages;
- design-system documentation.

A template-based approach may suit a straightforward need. Custom design may be justified when the brand, content or customer journey requires it. Ask what is being customised and why.

## Cost factor 3: content and copy

A website cannot be completed without accurate content. Clarify who provides:

- page copy;
- service and product information;
- images, video and graphics;
- team, company and trust information;
- testimonials or case studies with permission;
- policies and required notices;
- translations and qualified review;
- metadata and image alt text.

If the provider must research, structure, write, edit or migrate content, that work should appear in the scope. Client-supplied content still needs deadlines and an approval owner.

## Cost factor 4: content management system

A CMS allows approved users to manage selected content, but requirements vary.

Ask:

- Which content can be changed?
- Who receives administrator access?
- Is training or documentation included?
- How are roles and permissions handled?
- Who maintains the system and its dependencies?
- Can content be exported or migrated?
- What happens if the provider relationship ends?

Do not choose a CMS only because it is familiar. Evaluate security, maintenance, editing needs, ownership and the team's ability to use it responsibly.

## Cost factor 5: ecommerce requirements

Ecommerce scope can change with:

- product and category structure;
- variants, stock and pricing logic;
- customer accounts;
- checkout requirements;
- payment-provider integration;
- delivery, collection or fulfilment processes;
- discounts or promotions;
- tax and invoice requirements;
- order notifications and administration;
- returns, privacy and commercial policies;
- migration from an existing catalogue.

Payment, tax, delivery and legal details can change and must be verified for the specific business before publication or implementation. Do not assume that every ecommerce integration is currently available or suitable.

## Cost factor 6: integrations and custom functionality

Websites may need to exchange information with:

- customer or sales systems;
- booking or scheduling tools;
- email or marketing platforms;
- payment and delivery providers;
- analytics and consent tools;
- maps, feeds or external data;
- authentication or member systems;
- internal operational software.

Integration effort depends on documentation, access, data quality, security and error handling. A provider should identify dependencies and explain what happens when an external service changes or fails.

## Cost factor 7: responsive and mobile experience

Responsive work is not simply shrinking a desktop design. Content hierarchy, navigation, forms, images, tables, interactions and touch targets may need different decisions on smaller screens.

Confirm that the scope includes:

- agreed device and browser testing;
- responsive page templates;
- usable navigation and forms;
- image and media handling;
- readable content hierarchy;
- performance review on relevant devices;
- accessible interaction states.

Do not accept “mobile-friendly” as the only requirement. Ask how it will be reviewed.

## Cost factor 8: SEO requirements

SEO-ready foundations may include:

- crawlable page structures;
- logical headings and content hierarchy;
- editable titles and descriptions;
- canonical handling;
- redirects during migration;
- sitemap and robots controls;
- structured data where appropriate;
- useful internal linking;
- image handling and alt text;
- performance and accessibility foundations;
- analytics and search-verification setup where included.

These foundations do not guarantee rankings. Ongoing research, content, authority, maintenance and competition also affect search visibility. Confirm whether the quotation covers technical foundations, content optimization, ongoing SEO or only selected items.

## Cost factor 9: hosting, domain and infrastructure

Initial website development and ongoing infrastructure should be separated.

Clarify:

- who registers and owns the domain;
- where the site is hosted;
- who owns the hosting account;
- renewal responsibilities;
- backups and recovery arrangements;
- email hosting boundaries;
- certificates and security configuration;
- expected support if infrastructure fails;
- what happens during handover.

Do not publish or rely on a fee without checking the current provider, plan and renewal terms.

## Cost factor 10: maintenance, security and support

Launch is not the end of website ownership. Ongoing work can include:

- software or dependency updates;
- backups and restore testing;
- security monitoring and access review;
- bug fixes;
- content changes;
- performance monitoring;
- analytics checks;
- integration maintenance;
- support and incident response.

Ask what maintenance is included, optional or the client's responsibility. No provider can responsibly promise that a website will never experience a vulnerability or outage.

## Cost factor 11: analytics and measurement

Analytics scope should follow the business objective.

Define:

- meaningful website actions;
- forms, calls, bookings, purchases or downloads to record where applicable;
- consent and privacy responsibilities;
- who receives access;
- who checks whether measurement continues working;
- how website actions connect to offline sales or service processes;
- attribution limitations.

Tool installation alone does not create useful measurement. Events and reporting need clear definitions.

| Design | Template use, custom work, responsive states and revisions |
| Content | Writing, migration, images, translation and client inputs |
| CMS | Editing capability, roles, training, ownership and maintenance |
| Ecommerce | Catalogue, checkout, payments, fulfilment and administration |
| Integrations | Systems, dependencies, access, testing and failure handling |

A low total may exclude content, migration, integrations, maintenance or ownership that another proposal includes. Compare outputs and responsibilities, not only pages.

## Initial costs versus ongoing costs

### Initial project costs may include

- discovery and planning;
- content and sitemap work;
- design and development;
- content entry or migration;
- integrations;
- testing and launch;
- training and handover.

### Ongoing costs may include

- domain and hosting renewals;
- third-party services or licences;
- maintenance and security work;
- content updates;
- support;
- analytics or SEO activity;
- integration changes.

The exact responsibility and fee must be verified in the proposal. This article deliberately provides no standard amount.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a brochure website always cheaper than ecommerce?

Not automatically. Ecommerce usually adds operational functionality, but a custom information site can also require complex design, content, integrations or migration. Compare the actual scope.

### Does the number of pages determine website cost?

It is one factor. Unique templates, content complexity, custom functions, integrations, languages, migration and approval work can matter more than a raw page count.

### Are domain and hosting included in development?

It depends on the provider. Confirm registration, ownership, hosting, renewals, backups, email boundaries and handover separately.

### Does an SEO-ready website guarantee rankings?

No. Technical and content foundations make the site more suitable for search work, but rankings also depend on relevance, competition, authority, ongoing content and other factors.

### Who should own the website and accounts?

Ownership should be agreed in writing. Confirm code, design, content, domain, hosting, CMS, analytics, licences, data and access before work begins.

### What maintenance is needed after launch?

It depends on the technology and integrations. Typical responsibilities may include updates, backups, security, content, performance, analytics and support. The proposal should assign each one.
